Research

Working papers

Multi-Market Firms: Markups, Size, and Misallocation (JMP)

Abstract: Large firms are believed to hold the most market power, and models of oligopoly attribute a firm’s markup to its share of a single market. Yet firms often sell in many markets. Using market research data that record the sales of consumer goods firms in every product category and country they sell in, matched to firm financials, I document three facts. First, markups are flat in a firm’s average market share. Second, markups rise with the number and the size of the markets a firm is in. Third, larger markets have more competitors, lower top shares and a lower Herfindahl-Hirschman index. Together, the facts suggest that a firm’s markup depends on which markets it sells in rather than on its share of them. I rationalize them in an oligopoly model in which multi-market firms choose which markets to enter and invest in customer attachment, which lowers the elasticity of the demand they face. The return to attachment scales with market size while its cost rises less than proportionally, so markups rise with market size at any share, while entry makes larger markets more crowded. In the calibrated model, 85% of the variance of markups across firm-market pairs is between markets, so firm size is a poor guide to markups. As a result, a subsidy that rises with firm size removes only about 10% of misallocation, while one that varies with market size removes nearly 90%.

Labor Force Growth and Firm Dynamics in Europe

Abstract: Firm entry has declined in Europe in parallel with the United States. For the U.S., Hopenhayn, Neira, and Singhania (2022) attribute the decline to slower labor force growth. I show that the same explanation holds in Europe. Using a new dataset on firm dynamics for twelve European economies, I compute the entry rate that labor force growth implies from firm age profiles of size and survival, which pin down firm entry without solving a structural model. The predicted entry rate tracks the data in Europe as closely as in the United States, with a root mean squared error of 0.7 percentage points for the EU aggregate against 1.6 for the U.S., suggesting that firm creation in Europe responds to the same demographic forces. Unless its labor force grows faster, Europe’s entry rate will stay near its historical low.

Work in progress

Markups and Price Discovery, with Niccolò Zaccaria

Abstract: A firm’s profit-maximizing markup depends on its demand elasticity, which models of markups tend to assume the firm knows. We document how firms reprice their products and show that this repricing behaves like a search for that elasticity. Using scanner data, with the supermarket’s private label as a cost benchmark, we follow product-level markups over each product’s life. Products with higher revenue reprice more often. Price changes are larger, and more likely to be increases, the farther a product is below its settled markup. We rationalize this behavior in a model in which firms learn their demand elasticity from sales at the prices they post. In the model, pricing errors lower the aggregate markup, so better pricing raises it with demand held fixed. We use the model to quantify this rise and its welfare implications, since better pricing transfers surplus from consumers to firms.
